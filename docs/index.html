Rover build log, September to October 2026 · CAD and notes on GitHub

A survey rover from a GalaxyRVR kit

We took SunFounder's six-wheel GalaxyRVR, dropped the solar panel, and put a Raspberry Pi 5, a 12 MP wide camera and a 360° lidar on two printed PLA plates. A laptop drives it over Wi-Fi, and it can map a room and drive itself to a point on the map.

It is a learning vehicle for surveying a crawl space. Everything here was built and measured on a hobby budget.

Printed parts

Both parts were printed in PLA on a Bambu Lab A1 mini. The lower plate takes the solar panel's place and bolts into the chassis's existing threaded holes. A 21 mm channel under the plate clears the chassis strip and keeps the top flat.

MaterialPLA
Walls, infill4 wall loops, 15 % gyroid

OrientationLower plate top face down, so the nut pockets and channel print facing up. Carrier flat.
FastenersNo heat-set inserts: captive M3 nuts, countersunk screws from below, and the EP-0225's own standoffs

The files here are rebuilt from the build's measured specification by cad/build_parts.py (FreeCAD). They include one change made after printing: the camera bracket holes sit 5 mm farther along the plate's width, where we re-drilled them to clear the Pi's power cable. Two walls are thinner than our own 2.5 and 3 mm minimums: between two nut pockets and the channel (1.1 mm), and the roof over two Pi screw pockets inside the channel (2.6 mm).

Assembly

  1. Build the kit with SunFounder's assembly video, but leave off the solar panel and its mount. Its space becomes the payload deck.
  2. Print the lower plate and the lidar carrier.
  3. Press four M3 nuts into the hex pockets under the plate, for the controller board.
  4. Fit the camera bracket and the four standoff bases with M3 countersunk screws pointing up from under the plate, nuts on top. Build each standoff stack to 45 mm.
  5. Mount the Pi with the EP-0225 on it: four M2.5 button heads up through the plate into the EP-0225's standoffs. Do this before the plate goes on the chassis, which covers those screw heads.
  6. Mount the R3 board and shield under where the lidar will be, with M3 screws from the top into the captive nuts.
  7. Bolt the plate to the chassis with four M3 screws into the chassis threads. The channel underneath sits over the chassis strip, and the 8 mm hole clears its center screw.
  8. Fit the lidar to the carrier with four M2.5 screws from below, then screw the carrier onto the standoffs from above.
  9. Connect the camera to the Pi's CAM/DISP 0 port with a 22-pin Pi 5 ribbon. Never plug it into the kit's ESP32 camera connector: it looks similar and is not compatible.
  10. Wire it up: lidar USB adapter to a Pi USB 2 port, wrapped in film and taped where it touches the chassis; R3 to the Pi with the modified cable; power bank to the EP-0225's USB-C input.
  11. Before every drive: both batteries strapped down, loose cable coiled away from the wheels and suspension, and nothing in the lidar's scan plane.

The main parts before mounting

The USB cable we cut

The Pi sends drive commands to the R3 board over USB. But the R3's USB port is also powered by the rover's battery, through the shield. With the rover switched on and nothing else connected, a USB meter on the R3's cable read 5.13 V. Plugged into the Pi, that cable would have tied the rover's 5 V to the Pi's own supply, and current could flow back into the Pi.

So on a spare USB-A to USB-B cable we opened the jacket and cut the red wire, the 5 V line (VBUS). Both cut ends were insulated and the section taped closed. White (D−), green (D+) and black (ground) stay connected: the two boards share data and ground, never power.

How we checked it

Voltage with a POWER-Z KM003C USB meter; the wires with a multimeter in continuity (beep) mode, cable unplugged at both ends.

Software

Plain Python, no ROS. Three computers, each with one job:

  • R3 board: small custom firmware. It moves only after an ARM command, and a drive command lapses unless the next one arrives in time; a watchdog stops the motors if the link goes quiet.
  • Raspberry Pi: records the camera and lidar, and passes drive commands to the R3 only while its own health checks pass.
  • Laptop: the operator's page and PS4 controller, and the navigation: a lidar map built from a driven recording, a particle filter to find the rover on it, and A* to plan a path.

The code is not in this repository yet.

  6. Sep 13Five-minute run driving with camera and lidar recording. The SD card started throwing write errors.
  7. Sep 17Camera recalibrated with tilted views of a printed target: the first calibration's focal length was about 5 % too high.
  8. Sep 18First drive to a point on the map by itself: three of three goals, 1.3 to 1.7 m away, reached within 100 mm (77, 66 and 53 mm by tape), at 20 % power with a hand on the dead-man key.
  9. Oct 12First drives outdoors, over a phone hotspot with a proportional PS4 stick: two sessions, 222 s of driving, no faults.

Lessons

  • Measure before you connect two boards. A USB port can be a power source. Five minutes with a meter found the back-feed above.
  • The SD card was the weakest part. Continuous camera and lidar recording stalled the first card and then gave write errors. We now record to RAM and copy off at the end, and ordered the official Raspberry Pi card to replace it.
  • A single-plane lidar is blind below its plane. At 183 mm it missed a 178 mm cylinder and a chair's star base. We mark those as keep-out zones on the map by hand.
  • Without wheel encoders, the motion model is a guess. The rover drove 29 % faster than its model said, and overshot every goal by 51 to 64 mm along its heading.
  • Tilt the calibration target. Our first camera calibration used almost straight-on views and came out 5 % off; only a cross-check against the lidar showed it.
